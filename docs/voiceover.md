# Voice-over script

Record this on your phone in a quiet room, phone about a hand's width from your mouth. Read it at a calm talking pace. One take per paragraph is fine, and leave a second of silence between paragraphs so they can be cut apart. Send the recordings as they are (m4a or mp3).

Target length is 2 minutes 35 seconds. The limit is 3 minutes.

---

**1. The label (0:00)**

"Take two tablets by mouth twice daily." Researchers showed that label to 395 adults. Among those who struggle with reading, seven in ten could say it back. Only one in three could count out the pills.

**2. The millions (0:14)**

A hospital discharge sheet is a whole page of labels like that. Thirty-five million hospital stays a year in the United States end with one. Sixty-one million Americans are over sixty-five. Twenty-nine million speak English less than very well. Around the world, seven hundred and thirty-nine million adults cannot read.

**3. Pictures work (0:34)**

Pictures fix this. In one trial, picture instructions cut parents' dosing mistakes from forty-eight percent to five. So why doesn't every patient get pictures? Because someone has to draw them.

**4. The image model (0:48)**

An image model can. So I asked one to draw a single spoonful of medicine. It drew eight. Across 168 doses, it got the number of pills wrong 83 times. On a medicine card, that picture is dangerous.

**5. Concord (1:05)**

This is Concord. It turns a discharge sheet into picture cards, and every picture has to pass a reading test. Two models from two different companies look at it with no hint, and say what they see. Here the model was asked for a swollen foot. Both readers saw a red sock. Thrown away, and drawn again.

**6. How I built it (1:28)**

Building it took three steps. I fine-tuned the image model on seventy open pictograms, so every card shares one style. I drew 540 pictures and had every one read back. Then I took counting away from the model. It draws one pill, the readers confirm it is one, and code repeats it. Sixteen checked pictures now build over a hundred thousand different medicine cards, and the count on every one is exact.

**7. Languages (1:55)**

A picture needs no translation. The words around it do. So Concord speaks seventy languages, and it writes the medicine's name in the patient's own script. I checked that too: more than a hundred and twenty thousand medicine names, each one read back by a second model.

**8. Try it (2:15)**

It is live, free, and needs no account. Paste a sheet or take a photo, pick a language, then print the cards or have them read aloud.

**9. Close (2:27)**

Concord. Visual discharge instructions, verified before the patient sees them.
